## How does your product get better as people use it?

Every won call teaches Q a new move, and every seller on Q has it within seconds. Claude finds the seller's own line that turned the buyer, makes it a reusable move and credits them by name. On one call, another seller's card showed "New move learned from John · Los Angeles" 8 seconds after the win, and a move learned selling a pen showed up ten minutes later on a restaurant software call as "Compare $160 to four Sunday hours." Moves rank by Bayesian win rate, 15% of nudges test new ones, losing moves retire, every loss leaves a lesson, and Q grades its own nudges after every call.

## Tell us about your design philosophy & the design decisions you are most proud of

A sales call leaves you one glance, so Q is designed to be understood in one. My proudest decision: the nudge is the hero, short and big enough to read mid-sentence, with credit, situation and buyer type small beneath. Tango, my own design system, is dark monochrome glass; the Q logo is one glowing ring whose light speeds up when someone talks, with color only at its edges, like a prism. The card always shows it's listening ("Hearing John…", "Thinking…"). Nudges dim after a minute so an old one never looks current, and amber means only the clock's last stretch or a misfire. One system runs across the card, playbook, call report and royalties page.

## How does your agent know it is failing? What counts as a failure? What guardrails have you put in place?

Q's worst failures are silent, so it audits itself after every call. How it knows: Claude Sonnet 5.5 grades each nudge as helped, neutral or hurt from what the buyer did next, with a one-line reason in the call report. What counts as failure: a nudge that hurt, or a move that keeps losing. Grading is strict: unused nudges are neutral, and a win never makes a nudge helpful. Guardrails: one verdict moves ranking by at most 0.2; a move that hurts at least twice, and over twice as often as it helps, is flagged "Often misfires" and retires; Q never exploits insecurity, grief or money trouble, never pushes someone struggling, and never invents a price or claim.

## How is your product (or products!) going to evolve to be worth $1B rapidly?

Anyone can call the same model; nobody can copy which moves actually closed, and that record compounds with every call. Q starts with startup and SMB SaaS reps on Meet, where a $39 or $99 seat is a card swipe, not a procurement cycle. Then high-ticket remote closers, then insurance, financial services and real estate as Q reaches Zoom, Teams and phone dialers. At about $0.12 per 15-minute call on Q's standard model, seats keep 66% to 87% after model cost. Royalties, 10% of revenue, pay closers whose moves win on other sellers' calls, so the best keep teaching the network. 103,000 seats at an $81 blend is $100M ARR: a $1B company at 10x.
